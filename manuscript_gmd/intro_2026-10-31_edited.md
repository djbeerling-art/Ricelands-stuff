# 1 Introduction (edited 31 Oct 2026; changes listed at the end)

Rice paddies emit about 32 Tg CH₄ yr⁻¹, roughly 9 % of direct anthropogenic CH₄ emissions (Saunois et al., 2025), and also emit N₂O associated with nitrogen fertilisation and soil redox cycling. Under continuous flooding, N₂O emissions are generally small (Akiyama et al., 2005), but they can increase during drainage and rewetting as fertiliser-derived nitrogen undergoes nitrification and denitrification. Alternate wetting and drying (AWD) is increasingly used to reduce CH₄ emissions, although it can increase N₂O emissions under some conditions (Kritee et al., 2018). Any change to paddy management must therefore be judged by its combined effect on both gases.

Enhanced rock weathering (EW) involves applying crushed silicate rock, typically basalt, to agricultural soils to accelerate mineral dissolution and atmospheric CO₂ removal. Weathering releases Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ and generates alkalinity, which can contribute to durable inorganic carbon removal through downstream transport or net formation of pedogenic carbonate (Hartmann et al., 2013 [check year]; Beerling et al., 2020). Global assessments identify India and China among the countries with the largest EW potential, reflecting their extensive areas of warm, intensively cultivated cropland (Beerling et al., 2020). Rice cultivation is therefore a promising setting for EW deployment. Paddy soils are warm and wet for much of the growing season, conditions that favour mineral dissolution, and puddling and tillage mix crushed rock into the soil. We estimate that about 72 Mha of rice cultivation lies within 250 km of mapped basic volcanic rock, so feedstock transport distances could be short (this study, from Hartmann and Moosdorf, 2012).

Applying silicate rock to rice paddies could combine improved crop productivity with CO₂ removal and lower greenhouse-gas emissions. Rice accumulates large amounts of silicon, and repeated harvesting can deplete plant-available Si, potentially limiting productivity where soil Si supply is inadequate (Ma and Yamaji, 2006 [add]). Silicate amendments can replenish this supply while releasing base cations that contribute to inorganic carbon removal. In a field experiment in northeast China, applying 5 t ha⁻¹ of crushed wollastonite increased rice grain yield by 12 %, raised straw Si concentrations by 40 % and increased soil inorganic carbon by an estimated 1.2 t CO₂ ha⁻¹ over a single growing season (Wang et al., 2024).

Commercial EW deployment in rice has already begun. Two companies, Mati Carbon and Alt Carbon, are applying crushed basalt in rice-growing regions of India. In Chhattisgarh, Mati Carbon has spread basalt on more than 600 smallholder farms covering about 810 ha; reported first-year yields increased by a median of 23 %, with an estimated CO₂ removal potential of 4 t CO₂ ha⁻¹ (Jordan et al., 2026). Alt Carbon applies basalt to tea estates and rice paddies in the Darjeeling and Terai regions and has had carbon-removal credits issued through the Isometric registry (ESG Post, 2026). These developments make it increasingly important to quantify the fate of weathering products in flooded soils and to establish whether silicate amendments alter CH₄ and N₂O emissions alongside inorganic carbon removal.

Small-scale experiments have reported lower CH₄ emissions after applications of basalt, silicate slag or wollastonite (Wang et al., 2014; Gong et al., 2026; Park et al., 2026), but the mechanisms are poorly understood and unquantified. EW typically raises soil pH, which could stimulate methanogenesis in acidic paddies, while Fe-bearing minerals and their weathering products may enhance Fe(III) reduction, which competes with methanogenesis. Dissolved Si released by weathering may also alter rice-root physiology and oxygen release. These processes interact with seasonal flooding, drainage and reoxidation, so responses depend on soil properties, amendment composition and water management. Existing experiments span relatively few soils and growing seasons, limiting their ability to resolve longer-term interactions between weathering, redox cycling and greenhouse-gas production. In addition, weathering-derived cations can remain on soil exchange sites for years before they contribute to exported alkalinity (Planavsky et al., 2026), so the timing of inorganic carbon removal may differ from that of any CH₄ response.

A process-based reactive-transport model (RTM) is needed to investigate these interactions across contrasting paddy environments and management regimes. Existing rice biogeochemistry models, including DNDC-Rice (Fumoto et al., 2008) and CH4MOD (Huang et al., 2004), simulate CH₄ and N₂O emissions with simplified representations of redox processes, but do not resolve silicate mineral dissolution or the associated aqueous geochemistry. Conversely, EW RTMs such as SCEPTER resolve mineral dissolution, cation exchange and soil solution chemistry, but were developed for aerated upland soils (Kanzaki et al., 2022, 2024).

Here we introduce Riceland SCEPTER v1.0, which extends SCEPTER to represent the coupled mineral, microbial and plant-mediated processes governing EW in rice soils under continuous flooding and AWD. The model adds redox-dependent microbial reactions, Fe and S cycling, CH₄ and N₂O production and transport, rice-associated processes and seasonal flooding, drainage and fallow periods (Sect. 2). We verify the model against PHREEQC and element mass balances (Sect. 2.7), calibrate it and evaluate it against published silicate-amendment experiments (Sect. 3), and use ten-year simulations across six contrasting rice-soil archetypes to quantify how basalt application alters greenhouse-gas emissions, the lag between cation release and inorganic carbon removal, and the uncertainty contributed by the empirical Si–root feedback (Sect. 4).
